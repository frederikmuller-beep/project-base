## 1. Purpose

This Playbook defines how BASE turns product value into a durable business. It connects market selection, positioning, pricing, acquisition, onboarding, retention, sales, support, partnerships, measurement, and operating cadence.

The Playbook contains hypotheses where evidence is still limited. A hypothesis must not be represented as a proven commercial fact.

## 2. Commercial thesis

Olympic weightlifting is the beachhead, not because it is the largest fitness market, but because it concentrates a high-value problem: technically demanding training, frequent coach-athlete coordination, measurable plans and outcomes, and weak generic tooling.

BASE wins first by becoming indispensable to a small, well-defined community. It expands only after retention and willingness to pay demonstrate that the core workflow matters.

## 3. Ideal customer profiles

### ICP 1 — Independent weightlifting coach

- Coaches approximately 5–40 active athletes.
- Uses spreadsheets, messaging, paper, or multiple apps.
- Writes individualized or semi-individualized programming.
- Feels administrative friction and incomplete athlete feedback.
- Can influence the tool used by athletes.
- Has authority and budget to test a low-cost product.

This is the recommended initial economic buyer.

### ICP 2 — Serious coached athlete

- Trains at least three times weekly.
- Values programming quality and progress.
- Wants fast logging and clear daily decisions.
- May pay personally but is easier to retain when the coach participates.

### ICP 3 — Weightlifting club

- Has several coaches and a recurring athlete base.
- Wants a consistent programming and review process.
- May need shared ownership, coach permissions, and club reporting.

Clubs are a second-stage segment after the individual coach workflow is proven.

### Not yet ideal

- Casual gym users seeking generic workouts.
- Large federations requiring procurement, national reporting, and custom integrations.
- Rehabilitation clinics or customers expecting medical decision support.
- Sports outside BASE's validated programming ontology.

## 4. Buyer and user map

| Role | Primary value | Main objection | Proof required |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coach/buyer | Time saved and better athlete oversight | Loss of control or migration effort | Faster review, explainable adjustments, athlete adoption |
| Athlete/user | Clear plan and effortless logging | Another app and more administration | Immediate usefulness during training |
| Club owner/buyer | Consistency and retention | Cost, onboarding, permissions | Coach adoption and operational visibility |

## 5. Positioning

### Category

Coach-led strength-training operating system.

### Positioning statement

For serious weightlifting coaches and athletes who need a better way to plan, adapt, and review training, BASE brings the daily plan, readiness, explainable adjustment, and session log into one focused workflow. Unlike generic fitness trackers or disconnected spreadsheets, BASE preserves coach control and makes every important change visible.

### Message hierarchy

1. Know what to do today.
2. Adjust with a reason—not a guess.
3. Log without leaving the workout.
4. Keep the coach in control.
5. Learn from the full training loop.

Avoid leading with “AI.” Lead with the user outcome. AI is supporting evidence only when it creates an observable benefit.

## 6. Value propositions

### Coach value

- Less time collecting and reconstructing training context.
- Faster view of plan adherence and exceptions.
- Clear control over automated adjustment boundaries.
- Reusable programming and a reliable history.
- Better conversations with athletes based on shared facts.

### Athlete value

- One clear daily plan.
- Responsible readiness support.
- Fast set logging.
- Visible reasoning and easy override.
- A training history that improves future decisions.

### Club value

- Consistent workflow across coaches.
- Easier athlete onboarding and continuity.
- Reduced dependence on isolated spreadsheets.
- Foundation for quality assurance and aggregated learning.

## 7. Offer design

BASE should initially sell a complete workflow, not a collection of feature add-ons.

### Pilot offer

- Fixed pilot period of 6–12 weeks.
- Founder-led onboarding.
- Defined athlete/coach cohort.
- Weekly feedback touchpoint.
- Clear success criteria established before start.
- No long-term lock-in.
- Explicit statement that the product is a pilot and not medical advice.

### Initial paid product hypothesis

- **Athlete:** personal training workflow, potentially included through a coach.
- **Coach:** base subscription plus an active-athlete allowance.
- **Club:** shared workspace with coach seats and athlete capacity.

Exact packaging remains a hypothesis until willingness-to-pay interviews and paid experiments are completed.

## 8. Pricing method

Pricing must be derived from value and tested behavior, not competitor averages alone.

Use this sequence:

1. Measure current workflow cost in coach time, athlete friction, and missed information.
2. Interview users using price-sensitivity and trade-off questions.
3. Present real paid pilot offers—not only hypothetical questions.
4. Test a small number of simple price points.
5. Measure conversion, activation, support cost, retention, and objection patterns.
6. Change packaging only when evidence identifies the constraint.

Potential Danish-market test ranges—not approved prices:

- Athlete: DKK 59–129 per month.
- Coach: DKK 249–699 per month depending on active athletes.
- Club: custom pilot price until usage and support requirements are understood.

Discounts must have a reason, expiry, and owner. Lifetime deals are prohibited unless explicitly approved as a financing decision.

## 9. Go-to-market sequence

### Phase 1 — Founder discovery

- Recruit 5–10 coaches for structured problem interviews.
- Observe their current planning and review workflow.
- Recruit 10–30 athletes through participating coaches.
- Run the existing 10–14 day prototype test.
- Identify repeated blockers, trust failures, and willingness-to-change signals.

### Phase 2 — Design-partner pilot

- Select 2–5 coaches with urgent problems and strong follow-through.
- Agree on a 6–12 week pilot scorecard.
- Onboard a bounded athlete cohort.
- Conduct weekly review and ship only evidence-backed improvements.
- Ask for payment or a signed conversion commitment before declaring value proven.

### Phase 3 — Paid beta

- Offer a standard coach plan.
- Use case studies and referrals from successful pilots.
- Build a repeatable onboarding flow.
- Track cohort retention and support cost before increasing acquisition.

### Phase 4 — Club motion

- Target clubs already containing successful individual coaches.
- Sell standardization and continuity, not executive dashboards.
- Add permissions and reporting only for demonstrated club needs.

## 10. Distribution channels

Prioritize channels with trust and domain density:

1. Founder network and direct coach outreach.
2. Weightlifting clubs and training camps.
3. Coach referrals and athlete invitations.
4. Educational content built from real coaching problems.
5. Partnerships with courses, events, and federations after product proof.
6. Search and paid acquisition only when activation and retention are understood.

BASE must not scale paid acquisition into a leaky retention funnel.

## 11. Discovery and sales process

### Qualification

Confirm:

- number and type of athletes;
- current planning/logging workflow;
- repeated operational pain;
- decision maker and budget authority;
- willingness to change behavior;
- pilot timing and success criteria;
- constraints involving privacy, safety, or integrations.

### Discovery questions

- Walk me through the last session you had to adjust.
- Where did the plan live, and where did the result live?
- What information arrived too late?
- How much time do you spend reviewing athletes each week?
- What would make you distrust a readiness recommendation?
- What would need to happen for you to pay and keep using BASE?

### Demo rule

Demonstrate the buyer's actual workflow: publish a plan, complete readiness, show a transparent adjustment, log a set, and review the result as coach. Do not use a feature tour as the primary sales narrative.

### Pilot close

A pilot agreement should define cohort, duration, responsibilities, data handling, support channel, success measures, price or conversion commitment, and final decision date.

## 12. Onboarding playbook

### Coach onboarding

1. Confirm goals and current workflow.
2. Configure account, athletes, units, and adjustment policy.
3. Import or manually create the first representative week.
4. Publish one session together.
5. Review how recommendations and overrides work.
6. Confirm what athletes and coaches can see.
7. Schedule the first success check.

### Athlete onboarding

1. Accept coach relationship and data terms.
2. See today's plan in under five minutes.
3. Complete one readiness check.
4. Log one working set.
5. Finish the session and understand what the coach receives.

Time-to-first-value is the time from account access to the first meaningfully completed training action.

## 13. Activation and retention

### Coach activation

A coach is activated when they publish a real session, at least one athlete starts it, and the coach reviews the resulting record.

### Athlete activation

An athlete is activated when they view an assigned plan, make a readiness decision, and log meaningful training.

### Retention loops

- A completed session improves the coach's next decision.
- A coach update creates a relevant next session for the athlete.
- Reused templates reduce future programming work.
- Visible history increases switching cost through utility, not lock-in.

Retention messages must point to real pending value. Notifications must not manufacture urgency.

## 14. Customer success

During pilot, every customer has:

- a named owner at BASE;
- a documented success plan;
- weekly or biweekly usage review;
- a single support channel;
- a log of blockers and requested outcomes;
- a scheduled conversion or stop decision.

Support requests are categorized as bug, usability issue, missing capability, education, safety/privacy concern, or commercial request. Safety and privacy concerns receive immediate escalation.

## 15. Evidence and experiments

Each commercial experiment records:

- hypothesis;
- target segment;
- offer and price;
- channel;
- sample and duration;
- leading and lagging measures;
- decision rule;
- result and next action.

Examples:

- Coaches will invite at least five athletes after one guided setup.
- At least 30% of qualified design partners will accept a paid pilot.
- Coaches who review athlete sessions weekly will retain better than those who do not.

Do not change price, offer, audience, and onboarding simultaneously when the goal is to learn which variable matters.

## 16. Business scorecard

### Product value

- activation rate by role;
- weekly active athletes and coaches;
- sessions started/completed/logged;
- recommendation trust and override behavior;
- week-4 and week-12 cohort retention;
- coach time saved or review clarity.

### Commercial performance

- qualified opportunities;
- pilot acceptance rate;
- paid conversion;
- monthly recurring revenue;
- average revenue per paying account;
- gross and net revenue retention;
- churn reason;
- support cost per active account.

### Unit economics

- customer acquisition cost by channel;
- gross margin after infrastructure and variable support;
- contribution margin;
- CAC payback period;
- lifetime-value range based on observed—not assumed—retention.

Metrics must be segmented by athlete, coach, and club motion.

## 17. Operating cadence

### Weekly

- Review product health, active pilots, critical feedback, incidents, and next experiments.
- Speak directly with at least one coach or athlete.
- Decide one thing to start, stop, and continue.

### Monthly

- Review cohort activation and retention.
- Reconcile revenue, cash, runway, and commitments.
- Review pipeline quality and lost-deal reasons.
- Revisit top product and commercial assumptions.

### Quarterly

- Evaluate strategic gates from the Founder's Blueprint.
- Confirm ICP, positioning, packaging, and channel priority.
- Review privacy, safety, and operational risk.
- Reallocate capital and founder time.

## 18. Partnerships

Partnerships are pursued only when they provide credible distribution, domain authority, essential data, or user value.

Every partnership requires a named user outcome, commercial model, data responsibilities, brand implications, exit path, and measure of success. BASE must not build bespoke integrations in exchange for vague exposure.

## 19. Brand and communication rules

- Demonstrate the workflow; do not promise guaranteed performance.
- Use athlete and coach stories only with explicit permission.
- Distinguish pilot results from generalizable evidence.
- Avoid medical language and fear-based claims.
- State what BASE cannot do as clearly as what it can.
- Treat trust, coach authority, and explainability as sales advantages.

## 20. Financial discipline

Maintain a rolling 18-month cash view with base, constrained, and growth cases. Every material cost must map to compliance, reliability, customer value, validated acquisition, or a defined learning objective.

Do not add fixed headcount to solve a problem that has not repeated. Do not accept enterprise revenue whose custom requirements materially fork the core product without founder approval.

## 21. Failure and stop rules

Pause or change the strategy when:

- users praise the concept but do not return;
- coaches will test but not invite real athletes;
- athletes log only under researcher supervision;
- trust scores remain low after explanation improvements;
- support cost makes the price unsustainable;
- acquisition grows while activation or retention declines;
- custom requests pull BASE away from the primary training loop.

A stopped experiment is useful when the learning is recorded and changes the next decision.

## 22. Twelve-month validation roadmap

### Months 0–2

Complete prototype testing, interview coaches, define the readiness policy review, and select design partners.

### Months 3–5

Deliver the persistent pilot MVP, onboard design partners, and measure activation, trust, and workflow friction.

### Months 6–8

Run paid pilots, improve coach workflow, test packaging, and establish week-4 retention evidence.

### Months 9–12

Launch a bounded paid beta, build a referral motion, evaluate club demand, and decide whether the business has earned investment in scale.

Timeline follows evidence and product safety, not calendar pressure.

## 23. Acceptance criteria

The Playbook is ready for approval when the ICP and buyer are explicit, the offer and pricing are framed as testable hypotheses, product and commercial metrics share definitions, the pilot has success and stop rules, customer responsibilities are operational, and the plan does not rely on unsupported market or performance claims.
